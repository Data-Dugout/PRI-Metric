Methodology

How the score is built

The formula, the scaling, and the data behind every PRI score.

The formula

PRI is the average of four offensive metrics, built with a backbone on expected stats. Each comparing a hitter's actual outcome to their expected outcome, scaled against that season's full range of hitters.

PRI = 14 [ MAXBA − (BA − xBA) RANGEBA + MAXSLG − (SLG − xSLG) RANGESLG + MAXwOBA − (wOBA − xwOBA) RANGEwOBA + MAXBABIP − BABIP RANGEBABIP ]

* All players with 100+ plate appearances that season are included.

A high score means the player under-produced relative to their skill (unlucky, likely to progress). A low score means they over-produced (lucky, likely to regress).

What each part of the formula means

If you've never seen a formula like this before, here's what each piece is actually doing, using BA as the example (SLG and wOBA work the same way):

  • xBA (Expected Batting Average) — a Statcast metric that measures the likelihood a batted ball becomes a hit, based on its exit velocity, launch angle, and the hitter's sprint speed.
For example, if a player crushes a ball 110 mph off the bat into the gap, but a center fielder makes a diving catch — the actual batting average on that play is .000, but the expected average could be .650, since that exact batted ball results in a hit about 65% of the time.
  • BA − xBA (Batting Average minus Expected Batting Average) — the gap between what a hitter actually batted and what their contact quality says they should have batted. A positive number means they hit better than their contact deserved — they got lucky. A negative number means they hit worse than their contact deserved — they got unlucky.
Example: a hitter batting .300 with an xBA of .250 has a difference of +.050 — a lucky season, since the actual result outran what the quality of contact predicted.
  • MAXBA — the single biggest (most positive) BA−xBA gap recorded by any qualified hitter that season. In other words, however lucky the luckiest hitter in baseball was that year.
  • RANGEBA — the distance between that unluckiest hitter and the luckiest hitter that season (MAX minus MIN). It's the full spread of "luck" that actually happened across the league that year.
  • BABIP (Batting Average on Balls in Play) — The hitter's batting average on balls in play. The league average can vary year to year, but normally sits around .300. This is scaled versus the MIN and MAX from the relevant year.

Putting it together

Subtracting the difference between the actual and expected number from the MAX, then dividing by the RANGE, results in a simple number between a 0–100 scale. The unluckiest hitter in baseball lands near 100, the luckiest lands near 0, and everyone else falls somewhere in between.

Because MAX and RANGE are different every season (some years produce more extreme outliers than others), they have to be recalculated each year.
